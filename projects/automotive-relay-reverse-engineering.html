From Car Central Locking to an Arduino-Controlled Dual-Relay Interface

Reverse-engineering an undocumented automotive relay module using PCB inspection, continuity and diode tracing, and carefully current-limited live tests—then validating independent 5V transistor-drive operation.

12V Relays2SC1815 NPNArduino UnoPCB TracingCircuit DebuggingMultimeter

Validated: two relay coils and 5V transistor-based actuation. In progress: GPIO firmware and complete speaker switching verification.

Problem and goal

The salvaged board came from an automotive central-locking system. There was no connector harness, usable pinout, or readily confirmed relay-coil rating. The goal was to reuse its two existing electromechanical relays and their transistor drivers for independent microcontroller-controlled switching, with a possible speaker-selection application.

Available hardware

  • Two LARM RY-15-I relays on a shared PCB
  • Two C1815 GR-marked NPN transistors
  • Eight-pin connector and discrete diodes, resistors and capacitors (16V marked)
  • Arduino Uno, multimeter and approximately 13V battery
  • 220Ω resistors used for temporary current limiting

Constraints

  • Unknown supply and input polarity initially
  • High-current-capable battery rather than a lab power supply
  • No original vehicle loom or circuit diagram
  • Continuity mode preferred over resistance testing
  • Need to avoid applying battery voltage to Arduino GPIO

Reverse-engineering process

  1. Mapped the connector: photographed both sides of the PCB and soldered an eight-wire breakout harness. Numbered connector pins consistently from the component side.
  2. Separated contact and driver routing: followed heavier copper tracks into the relay contacts and thinner tracks into the transistor and diode circuitry.
  3. Identified transistor topology: read C1815 GR device markings, checked the 2SC1815 datasheet, and used diode mode to validate a roughly 0.6V base-emitter junction on the upper transistor.
  4. Traced relay protection: SD3 and SD4 had joined cathodes (banded ends). Each anode connected to its respective transistor's middle collector leg and relay-coil return. These observations are consistent with flyback diodes.
  5. Validated coils with limited current: started with five 220Ω resistors in series, then adjusted the arrangement as tests progressed. Both relays eventually clicked at roughly 9.2V across their coil circuits.
  6. Verified switching: for the upper relay, its output measured near 0V when off and about 10V when energised in the current-limited contact test.
  7. Validated transistor control: traced the emitters to connector pins 6 (upper Q1) and 5 (lower Q2). With both emitters referenced to common ground, the two relays were individually actuated by a 5V control signal supplied through a 440Ω series resistor. This was a manual Arduino 5V-pin test, not a completed GPIO firmware test.

Lessons and remaining work

Key skills exercised include component identification from device markings, verifying transistor terminal functions, tracing shared nets across a PCB, isolating relay coil behaviour from contact behaviour, using current-limited power for unknown electronics, and distinguishing verified measurements from inferred topology.

  • Complete and document the lower relay's ON-state contact test.
  • Test programmable Arduino D8/D9 HIGH/LOW operation with a separate base resistor for each transistor, rather than relying on the board's 5V supply pin.
  • Replace the experimental resistor-limited battery feed with an appropriately protected supply after confirming coil and component ratings.
  • Check amplifier type, speaker impedance and switching transients before connecting audio loads.
  • Produce a fully verified schematic and add clear PCB photographs to the evidence set.
Safety boundary: A 25Ah-class battery can supply substantial fault current. The resistor network used here was a temporary limited-current experiment, not a final protective device. Never connect the Arduino GPIO directly to 12V or a relay coil; always validate shared-ground and interface voltages first.
